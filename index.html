ODGA Virginia Datathon 2026 · Team Penny Pinchers · Housing affordability

Virginians in their late 20s and 30s mostly rent, and rate cuts alone will not change that.

We built a model of Virginia's 133 counties and cities from the datathon housing data. It asks who rents, what a median renter can afford to buy, and where new homes could go without cutting people off from jobs and transit.

Problem statement

Renting is the default for young households, and it costs them twice as much of their income.

Among households aged 25 to 34, rent. Of those renters, spend 30% or more of income on housing, compared with of owners the same age.

Buying is not the easy way out. At a 6.5% mortgage rate with 5% down, the median renter household can afford about of the median home value in its own locality, and only localities reach parity.

Supply is thin. Statewide, homeowner vacancy is and rental vacancy . Just of vacant units are on the market at all.

The datathon theme is making housing affordable. We ask two questions: do 25 to 40 year olds mostly rent, and what would put ownership within reach? And can growth move toward places with room without losing access to jobs and transit?

Hypotheses and what we are trying to say

Two claims, tested against the data.

Hypothesis 2 · Renters
People aged roughly 25 to 40 mostly rent.
Supported for ages 25 to 34

What we are saying: young households rent because buying is out of reach, not by preference alone, and help has to cover both the monthly payment and getting in the door.

  • of 25 to 34 households rent, and renters are burdened at nearly twice the rate of owners.
  • No rate and down-payment combination alone gets the typical renter to parity.
  • Rent relative to renter income is the one factor that stood out in our burden model (p = 0.03).

Limit: the age tables split at 25 to 34 and 35 to 64, so 35 to 40 is not isolated. Renter income covers all ages.

Hypothesis 1 · Density and access
Parts of Virginia are dense and crowded; growth can shift toward places with room while keeping access.
Partly supported

What we are saying: the tradeoff is real. Sparse places have the room, dense places have the access, and shifting growth only helps if access moves with it.

  • Half the state's households live on under 5% of its land.
  • Sparse places have more vacant capacity (13.3% vs 5.9%) but spend about 9 more points of income on transport.
  • Near the squeezed cities there is plenty of land, but transit use drops to near zero.

Limit: there is no persons-per-room measure, so crowding is proxied by density and household size, and the data show no link between the two.

Method, assumptions and limits

How we got the numbers.

Monthly rent is multiplied by 12 before it meets yearly income.

ACS median gross rent is a monthly figure and household income is annual. Every rent-to-income number in this atlas uses annual rent = monthly rent × 12. Pick a locality to see the steps.

Without the ×12 step, the statewide median would read 2.4% of income instead of 28.8%.

Limits

  • 133 localities is a small sample: results are associations, not causes.
  • Density, land and transport fields are 2019-era; burden, vacancy and income are ACS 2024.
  • Density and access correlate at 0.92, so their separate effects are unstable.
  • Margins of error are wide for small places such as Caroline and Buena Vista.
  • The density break in the burden curve is not firmly located (bootstrap 390 to 1,988).
  • Radar uses straight-line distance and ZIP-centre points; no commute data.
  • Age and vacancy data are for 2024 only.
